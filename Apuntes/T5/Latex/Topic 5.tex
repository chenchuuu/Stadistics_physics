\chapter{Introduction}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/intr.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

In Chapter 4, we examined the microcanonical ensemble, focusing on isolated systems at equilibrium. In Chapter 5, we introduced the canonical ensemble, where systems in equilibrium exchange energy with their surroundings. This ensemble allows us to comprehend the behaviour of systems with a fixed energy content interacting with a thermal reservoir. Now, a key question arises: can we design an ensemble that captures the essence of macroscopic systems, ones that not only exchange energy with the external environment, but also mass? This scenario forms the basis of the macrocanonical (or grand canonical) ensemble, an ensemble tailor-made for systems achieving both energy and material equilibrium with the exterior. To this end, let’s first resume the main conclusions on microcanonical and canonical ensembles.

\begin{itemize}
	\item The \textbf{microcanonical ensemble} is mathematically challenging to employ. Moreover, the concept of isolated physical systems is more of an idealisation than a reality. In practical terms, the study of systems interacting with their environment often holds greater significance. Experimentally, there is no instrument available to directly measure or control a system's energy. Instead, it is typically inferred from other observables such as pressure, temperature, among others. By contrast, fixing the temperature proves to be more straightforward—it is directly observable through a thermometer and easily manageable with a thermal bath.
	
	\item The \textbf{canonical ensemble} proves to be a significantly more versatile ensemble, offering immense utility in addressing a multitude of physical problems within closed systems. However, the canonical ensemble exhibits significant limitations when attempting to address various other physical and chemical problems: \textit{(1)} systems in equilibrium that exchange particles with the surroundings (open systems); \textit{(2)} systems in equilibrium where chemical reactions take place; and \textit{(3)} systems separated into phases in mutual coexistence (liquid-vapour, solid-vapour, etc.). These are all systems where the number of particles of each species is not constant.
\end{itemize}

\esp

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/canonical.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

Let’s consider an isolated system at equilibrium. This system is formed by two subsystems, A and B, that can exchange energy and mass, as shown in Fig. 6.1. If the chemical potential of A, $\mu_A$, equals the chemical potential of B, $\mu_B$, then there is no net mass exchange. However, if this is not the case, then there will be a mass transfer from A to B if $\mu_A > \mu_B$ or from B to A if $\mu_A < \mu_B$. We should note that this mass transfer is internal to the A+B system. In other words,

\esp

\begin{equation}
	N_A + N_B = \text{const} \rightarrow dN_A + dN_B = 0 \tag{6.1}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent Additionally, the total entropy change of the system reads

\esp

\begin{equation}
	dS = \left( \frac{\partial S}{\partial N_A} \right)_{N_B,U,V} dN_A + \left( \frac{\partial S}{\partial N_B} \right)_{N_A,U,V} dN_B = dN_A \left[ \left( \frac{\partial S}{\partial N_A} \right) - \left( \frac{\partial S}{\partial N_B} \right) \right] \tag{6.2}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent where the infinitesimal entropy change with mass is proportional to the chemical potential:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\mu = -T \left( \frac{\partial S}{\partial N} \right)_{U,V} \tag{6.3}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent Therefore, substituting Eq. 6.3 into Eq. 6.2, we obtain

\esp

\begin{equation}
	dS = -\frac{dN_A}{T}(\mu_A - \mu_B) \tag{6.4}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent Note that, if $\Delta\mu \equiv \mu_A - \mu_B > 0$, then $dN_A < 0$; by contrast, if $\Delta\mu < 0$, then $dN_A > 0$. In both cases, $dS > 0$ and $dS = 0$ if no mass transfer occurs, that is if $\Delta\mu = 0$.

\esp

\begin{center}
	% [Representación esquemática: A + B <=> C]
	\textbf{Figure 6.2:} System where a chemical reaction takes place and a product forms.
\end{center}

\esp

Now, let’s consider a system where the generic chemical reaction A + B = C occurs (see Fig. 6.2). At equilibrium, the chemical potentials of the three species are related as follows

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\mu_A + \mu_B = \mu_C \tag{6.5}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent Out of equilibrium, then products or reagents can form:

\esp

\begin{align}
	\mu_A + \mu_B > \mu_C &\implies \text{A + B} \rightarrow \text{C} \tag{6.6} \\
	\mu_A + \mu_B < \mu_C &\implies \text{C} \rightarrow \text{A + B} \tag{6.7}
\end{align}

\esp

In Fig. 6.3, we schematically show a representative member $S$ of the macrocanonical ensemble that exchange energy and mass with its surrounding reservoir $W$. The net mass exchange is zero at the thermodynamic equilibrium, when $T_s = T_w$ and $\mu_s = \mu_w$. In particular, $S$ is confined within rigid, permeable and diathermic walls that allow mass and energy transfer, but leave temperature ($T$), volume ($V$) and chemical potential ($\mu$) constant. To stress these conditions, the macrocanonical ensemble that we wish to design is also denoted as $(\mu, V, T)$ ensemble and the corresponding partition function as $\Xi = \Xi(\mu, V, T)$. Additionally, we will introduce the \textbf{macrocanonical potential}, also known as the grand canonical potential or the Helmholtz free energy in the macrocanonical ensemble, $J(\mu, V, T) = -kT \ln \Xi$, defined as:

\begin{equation}
	J = U - TS - \mu N = A - \mu N \tag{6.8}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent where $U$ is the internal energy, $S$ the entropy, $N$ the number of particles and $A$ the Helmholtz free energy. The minimisation of the macrocanonical potential at equilibrium conditions provides the most probable distribution of energy and particles for a given temperature, chemical potential, and volume.

\section{Design of the grand canonical ensemble}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/design.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

With reference to Fig. 6.4, the system $S$ is very large, but much smaller than $W$, that is $S \ll W$, and

\esp

\begin{equation}
	1 \ll N_s \ll N_w \tag{6.9}
\end{equation}

\esp

Additionally, the numerical density is basically constant across all $U$:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\frac{N_s}{V_s} \simeq \frac{N_w}{V_w} \tag{6.10}
\end{equation}

\esp

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/design2.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

If we disregard the interaction energy between $S$ and $W$, the energy of the "supersystem" $U$ reads

\esp

\begin{equation}
	E_u \simeq E_s + E_w, \quad \text{with } E_s \ll E_w \tag{6.11}
\end{equation}

\esp

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/partition.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

Given that the Hamiltonian of the super-system $U$ is separated into two parts ($S + W$), the Schrödinger equation also separates:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\hat{H}_s|\phi_s\rangle = E_s|\phi_s\rangle \tag{6.12}
\end{equation}

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\hat{H}_w|\phi_w\rangle = E_w|\phi_w\rangle \tag{6.13}
\end{equation}

\esp

where $E_s(N)$ is the energy of $S, E_w$ is the energy of $W$ and $|\phi_u\rangle = |\phi_s\rangle|\phi_w\rangle$. Given that $S$ has quantum numbers that are independent on $W$, it makes sense to estimate the probability, $P_{mN}$, that $S$ contains $N$ particles and is in the energy state $E_{mN}$. Since $W$ is much larger than $S$, we can also consider $W$ as an isolated system with energy $E_w = E_u - E_{mN}$ and $N_w = N_u - N$ particles. It follows that the number of accessible states in $U$ reads

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\Omega_u = \Omega_u(E_u, N_u; \Delta E) \tag{6.14}
\end{equation}

\esp

and the number of accessible states in $U$ compatible with $S$ containing $N$ particles and having energy $E_{mN}$, reads

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\Omega_w = \Omega_w(E_u - E_{mN}, N_u - N; \Delta E) \tag{6.15}
\end{equation}

\esp

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/partition2.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

We can then calculate the aforementioned probability in terms of the energy and number of particles of the reservoir $W$:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	P_{mN} = \frac{\text{favourable cases}}{\text{possible cases}} = \frac{\Omega_w(E_u - E_{mN}, N_u - N; \Delta E)}{\Omega_u(E_u, N_u; \Delta E)} \tag{6.16}
\end{equation}

\esp

Given that $E_{mN} \ll E_u$, we can develop a power series of $\Omega_w$. Nevertheless, since $\Omega_w$ increases exponentially with energy and converges slowly, we prefer to Taylor-expand the logarithm of $\Omega_w$ near the point $(E_u, N_u)$:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\ln \Omega_w(E_w, N_w; \Delta E) \simeq \ln \Omega_w(E_u, N_u; \Delta E) + \left( \frac{\partial \ln \Omega_w}{\partial E_w} \right)_{E_w=E_u} (E_w - E_u) + \left( \frac{\partial \ln \Omega_w}{\partial N_w} \right)_{N_w=N_u} (N_w - N_u) \tag{6.17}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent or equivalently

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\ln \Omega_w(E_w, N_w; \Delta E) \simeq \ln \Omega_w(E_u, N_u; \Delta E) - \underbrace{\frac{\partial \ln \Omega_w}{\partial E_w}}_{\beta=1/kT} E_{mN} - \underbrace{\frac{\partial \ln \Omega_w}{\partial N_w}}_{-\beta\mu} N \tag{6.18}
\end{equation}

\esp

where we used the fact that the thermal bath $W$ can be approximately treated as an isolated system and therefore within the microcanonical-ensemble framework. It follows that $S_w = k \ln \Omega_w$ and, from Eq. 6.3, we obtain the chemical potential in the above equation. Therefore,

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\Omega_w(E_u - E_{mN}, N_u - N; \Delta E) \simeq \Omega_w(E_u, N_u; \Delta E) e^{-\beta(E_{mN}-\mu N)} \tag{6.19}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent and the probability reads

\esp

\begin{equation}
	P_{mN} = \underbrace{\frac{\Omega_w(E_u, N_u; \Delta E)}{\Omega_u(E_u, N_u; \Delta E)}}_{\leq 1} e^{-\beta(E_{mN}-\mu N)} \tag{6.20}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent To calculate $P_{mN}$, we can impose the normalisation condition:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m} P_{mN} = 1 \tag{6.21}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent which gives

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m} \underbrace{\frac{\Omega_w(E_u, N_u; \Delta E)}{\Omega_u(E_u, N_u; \Delta E)}}_{\equiv 1/\Xi} e^{-\beta(E_{mN}-\mu N)} = 1 \tag{6.22}
\end{equation}

\esp

where the sum is over all possible states and number of particles of system $S$, without restriction. Rearranging, one obtains

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\Xi \equiv \frac{\Omega_u(E_u, N_u; \Delta E)}{\Omega_w(E_u, N_u; \Delta E)} = \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m} e^{-\beta(E_{mN} - \mu N)} \tag{6.23}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent Substituting this result into Eq. 6.20, we obtain

\esp

\begin{equation}
	P_{mN} = \frac{1}{\Xi} e^{-\beta(E_{mN} - \mu N)} \tag{6.24}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent where $\Xi = \Xi(\mu, T, V)$ is the \textbf{macrocanonical partition function} and $P_{mN}$ is the \textbf{macrocanonical probability}. Note that both $\Xi$ and $P_{mN}$ do not depend on $W$.

\esp

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/partitionqp.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/partitionqp2.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

In terms of operators:

\esp

\begin{align}
	\hat{\rho} &= \frac{1}{\Xi} e^{-\beta(\hat{H}-\mu\hat{N})} \tag{6.25} \\
	\Xi &= \text{Tr} \left[ e^{-\beta(\hat{H}-\mu\hat{N})} \right] \tag{6.26}
\end{align}

\esp

and the average value of a generic observable reads

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\langle b \rangle = \text{Tr} \left[ \hat{b}\hat{\rho} \right] = \frac{1}{\Xi} \text{Tr} \left[ \hat{b}e^{-\beta(\hat{H}-\mu\hat{N})} \right] \tag{6.27}
\end{equation}

\section{Connection with Thermodynamics}

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/thermo.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

In this section, our goal is understanding what the physical meaning of $\mu$ is and how $\Xi$, and more generally the macrocanonical ensemble, is related to Thermodynamics. In the $(\mu, V, T)$ ensemble, both $E$ and $N$ are not constant as the system exchanges energy and mass with the surroundings. Nevertheless, from a Thermodynamics perspective, we can still define their average values:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	U = \langle E \rangle = \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m} E_{mN} P_{mN} = \frac{1}{\Xi} \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m} E_{mN} e^{-\beta(E_{mN}-\mu N)} \tag{6.28}
\end{equation}

\esp

where $E_{mN}$ is also a function of the volume $V$. Let's define the \textbf{fugacity} as $\lambda \equiv e^{\beta\mu}$. Then Eq. 6.28 is modified as

\esp

\begin{equation}
	U = \frac{1}{\Xi} \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m} E_{mN} e^{-\beta E_{mN}} \lambda^N = -\frac{1}{\Xi} \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \lambda^N \sum_{m} \left. \frac{\partial e^{-\beta E_{mN}}}{\partial \beta} \right|_V = -\frac{1}{\Xi} \frac{\partial}{\partial \beta} \left[ \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \lambda^N \sum_{m} e^{-\beta E_{mN}} \right]_{\lambda, V} \tag{6.29}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent which can simply be written as

\esp

\begin{equation}
	U = -\frac{1}{\Xi} \left( \frac{\partial \Xi}{\partial \beta} \right)_{\lambda, V} = -\left( \frac{\partial \ln \Xi}{\partial \beta} \right)_{\lambda, V} \tag{6.30}
\end{equation}

\esp

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/thermo2.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

\noindent It then follows that

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\beta_1 = \beta_2 = \beta, \quad \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \mu \tag{6.36}
\end{equation}

\esp

This suggests that $\mu$ does not depend on the system size or, in other words, that it is an \textbf{intensive} property. It also follows that

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\Xi = \Xi_1 \Xi_2 \tag{6.37}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent or

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\ln \Xi = \ln \Xi_1 + \ln \Xi_2. \tag{6.38}
\end{equation}

\esp

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{IMG/thermo3.png}
\end{figure}

\esp

This result indicates that the property $\ln \Xi$ and hence also $J \equiv -kT \ln \Xi$, which we call macrocanonical potential, are \textbf{extensive} variables. In particular, the macrocanonical potential depends on temperature, chemical potential, and energy state of the system. Mathematically:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	J = J(\beta, \mu, \{E_{mN}\}) \tag{6.39}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent Its total differential can therefore be written as

\esp

\begin{equation}
	d(\beta J) = \left. \frac{\partial(\beta J)}{\partial \beta} \right|_{\mu, \{E_{mN}\}} d\beta + \left. \frac{\partial(\beta J)}{\partial \mu} \right|_{\beta, \{E_{mN}\}} d\mu + \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m} \left. \frac{\partial(\beta J)}{\partial E_{mN}} \right|_{\beta, \mu} dE_{mN} \tag{6.40}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent Let's calculate these three partial derivatives separately.

\esp

\noindent \textbf{I}

\esp

\begin{align}
	\left. \frac{\partial(\beta J)}{\partial \beta} \right|_{\mu, \{E_{mN}\}} &= -\frac{\partial \ln \Xi}{\partial \beta} = -\frac{1}{\Xi} \left. \frac{\partial \Xi}{\partial \beta} \right|_{\mu, \{E_{mN}\}} = \nonumber \\
	&= -\frac{1}{\Xi} \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m} (-E_{mN} + \mu N) e^{-\beta(E_{mN} - \mu N)} = \nonumber \\
	&= \frac{1}{\Xi} \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m} E_{mN} e^{-\beta E_{mN} + \beta \mu N} - \mu \frac{1}{\Xi} \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m} N e^{-\beta E_{mN} + \beta \mu N} = \nonumber \\
	&= \langle E \rangle - \mu \langle N \rangle = \nonumber \\
	&= U - \mu \langle N \rangle \tag{6.41}
\end{align}

\esp

\noindent \textbf{II}

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\left. \frac{\partial(\beta J)}{\partial \mu} \right|_{\beta, \{E_{mN}\}} = -\left. \frac{\partial \ln \Xi}{\partial \mu} \right|_{T,V} = -\beta \langle N \rangle \tag{6.42}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent \textbf{III}

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\left. \frac{\partial(\beta J)}{\partial E_{mN}} \right|_{\beta, \mu} = -\frac{1}{\Xi} \frac{\partial \Xi}{\partial E_{mN}} = -\frac{1}{\Xi} (-\beta) e^{-\beta E_{mN} + \beta \mu N} = \beta P_{mN} \tag{6.43}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent Therefore Eq. 6.40 is re-written as

\esp

\begin{equation}
	d(\beta J) = (U - \mu \langle N \rangle) d\beta - \beta \langle N \rangle d\mu + \beta \sum_{Nm} P_{mN} dE_{mN} \tag{6.44}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent where $\sum_{Nm} \equiv \sum_{N=0}^{\infty} \sum_{m}$. We now note that:

\esp

\begin{align}
	U d\beta &= d(U\beta) - \beta dU \nonumber \\
	-\mu \langle N \rangle d\beta - \beta \langle N \rangle d\mu &= -d(\beta \mu \langle N \rangle) + \beta \mu d\langle N \rangle \tag{6.45}
\end{align}

\esp

\noindent It follows that

\esp

\begin{equation}
	d(\beta J) = d(U\beta) - \beta dU - d(\beta \mu \langle N \rangle) + \beta \mu d\langle N \rangle + \beta \sum_{Nm} P_{mN} dE_{mN} \tag{6.46}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent and rearranging, we obtain

\esp

\begin{equation}
	d[\beta(U - \mu \langle N \rangle - J)] = \beta dU - \beta \mu d\langle N \rangle - \beta \sum_{Nm} P_{Nm} dE_{mN} \tag{6.47}
\end{equation}

\esp

Now, what is the physical meaning of the last term in the r.h.s. of the above equation? To answer this question, we first notice that we are assuming that $E_{mN} = E_{mN}(V)$ and, if the volume changes, then the energy will change accordingly. In other words, $dE_{mN}$ is a change in the energy levels due to a volume change $dV$. If we impose a volume change, keeping constant all the other variables, the internal energy will also change:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	U = \sum_{Nm} P_{Nm} E_{Nm} \rightarrow U' = \sum_{Nm} P_{Nm} E'_{Nm} \tag{6.48}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent If we now apply the First Law of Thermodynamics, we observe that

\esp

\begin{equation}
	dU = U' - U = \sum_{Nm} P_{Nm} dE_{Nm} = \sum_{Nm} P_{Nm} \left( \frac{\partial E_{Nm}}{\partial V} \right) dV = đ W = -pdV \tag{6.49}
\end{equation}

\esp

where $p$ stands for pressure. It becomes clear that

\esp

\begin{equation}
	-\sum_{Nm} P_{Nm} \left( \frac{\partial E_{Nm}}{\partial V} \right) = p, \quad \text{pressure} \tag{6.50}
\end{equation}

\esp

\noindent and

\esp

\begin{equation}
	\sum_{Nm} P_{Nm} dE_{Nm} = đW, \quad \text{mechanical work.} \tag{6.51}
\end{equation}

\esp

If we incorporate this result into Eq. 6.52, then we obtain

\esp

\begin{equation}
	d[\beta(U - \mu\langle N \rangle - J)] = \beta[dU - \mu d\langle N \rangle - đW] = \beta[dU + pdV - \mu d\langle N \rangle] \tag{6.52}
\end{equation}

\esp

Equivalently

\esp

\begin{equation}
	d \left[ \frac{1}{T}(U - \mu\langle N \rangle - J) \right] = \frac{1}{T}[dU + pdV - \mu d\langle N \rangle] \tag{6.53}
\end{equation}

\esp

Let's now apply the Second Law of Thermodynamics and obtain the Fundamental Equation of Thermodynamics, which combines the first and the second laws:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	đQ = TdS = dU + pdV - \mu dN \tag{6.54}
\end{equation}

\esp

Rearranging, we obtain the infinitesimal change of entropy:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	dS = \frac{1}{T}[dU + pdV - \mu dN] \tag{6.55}
\end{equation}

\esp

or the infinitesimal change of internal energy:

\esp

\begin{equation}
	dU = TdS - pdV + \mu dN \tag{6.56}
\end{equation}

\esp

Comparing Eq. 6.55 with Eq. 6.53, one observes that $\mu$ is indeed the chemical potential and that the argument of the derivative in the l.h.s. of Eq. 6.53 results to be

\esp

\begin{equation}
	U - \mu\langle N \rangle - J = TS \tag{6.57}
\end{equation}

\esp

or, rearranging, that

\esp

\begin{equation}
	J = U - TS - \mu\langle N \rangle \tag{6.58}
\end{equation}

\esp

which justifies the definition of macrocanonical potential for $J(\mu, T, V) = -kT \ln \Xi$. Additionally, from Eq. 6.56, we see that

\esp

\begin{equation}
	U = TS - pV + \mu N \tag{6.59}
\end{equation}

\esp

and finally

\esp

\begin{equation}
	J(\mu, T, V) = U - TS - \mu N = -pV = -p(T, \mu)V \tag{6.60}
\end{equation}

\esp

This last equation, combined with $J = -kT \ln \Xi$, is crucial to derive all the equations of Thermodynamics. More specifically:

\esp

\begin{align}
	S &= -\left. \frac{\partial J}{\partial T} \right|_{\mu, V} = kT \left. \frac{\partial \ln \Xi}{\partial T} \right|_{\mu, V} + k \ln \Xi, \quad \text{Entropy} \tag{6.61} \\
	p &= -\left. \frac{\partial J}{\partial V} \right|_{\mu, T} = kT \left. \frac{\partial \ln \Xi}{\partial V} \right|_{\mu, T} = \frac{kT}{V} \ln \Xi, \quad \text{Pressure} \tag{6.62} \\
	\langle N \rangle &= -\left. \frac{\partial J}{\partial \mu} \right|_{T, V} = kT \left. \frac{\partial \ln \Xi}{\partial \mu} \right|_{T, V}, \quad \text{Average number of particles} \tag{6.63}
\end{align}
